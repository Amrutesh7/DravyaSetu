Abstract: Lemongrass, a medicinal plant belonging to the family Poaceae. This medicinal plant contains
compounds that can control pathogens and enhance the resistance of herbal remedies against diseases caused
by pathogens. Lemongrass is commonly utilized in the preparation of herbal teas, non-alcoholic beverages,
baked goods, and confections. Additionally, the essential oil derived from lemongrass is widely used as a
fragrance in perfumes and cosmetics, including creams and soaps. The essential oil of lemongrass is rich in
citral, which is a valuable source to produce beta carotene and vitamin A, among other substances. It contains
considerable group of flavonoids, essential oils, phenolic compound, and other photochemical constituents
which possesses pharmacological activities such as anti-obesity, antibacterial, anti-fungal, anti-nociceptive,
anti-oxidant, anti-diarrheal, and anti-inflammatory properties which could enhance health. In addition,
lemongrass oil also contains essential minerals such as Fe, Zn, Mg, Na, K,Ca, Mn, and P but unfortunately, it
is not utilized in broad spectrum among rural & urban people. Keeping the importance of Lemongrass, an
attempt has been made to gather the information on it through a literature survey. The results are presented in
this chapter to bring attention towards its importance.
Keywords: Economic values, lemon grass, nutraceutical values

Introduction: Lemon grass, popularly known as citronella grass, is a plant species that
belongs to the family Poaceae and to the genus Cymbopogon. The genus Cymbopogon is
composed of about 140 species. Lemon grass is native to the semi-arid and tropical areas of
Asia, America, and Africa. It is native to Pakistan, India, and Sri Lanka. In India it is in
Western Ghats (Kerala, Maharashtra), Karnataka and the Tamil Nadu states other than foot
slopes of the Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh (Ranade and Thiagarajan 2015; Wifek et al.
2016). Several species of lemon grass such as Cymbopogon bombycinus, Cymbopogon
ambiguus, Cymbopogon obtectus, Cymbopogon refractus, Cymbopogon citratus,
Cymbopogon citrate, Cymbopogon nardus, Cymbopogon schoenanthus etc., found in

that grows in thick bunches from a strong base, reaching a height of about 3 meters with a
width of about 1 meter. In addition to its culinary usage, lemongrass offers a wide array of
medicinal benefits and is in high demand due to its antimicrobial properties (Wifek et al.
2016).
Description: It is a perennial monocotyledonous grass that can grow up to 6 feet tall and 4
feet wide (Figure 1). It grows in clumps. The culm is stout, erect, 1.8 to 2 meter high. It has
long, slender, drooping, bright green leaves 1.3-2.5 inches wide and 3 feet long and 3feet
(Figure 2). The leaves are simple, glaucous, linear tapering upwards and along the margins;
ligule very short; sheaths terete, those of the barren shoots widened and tightly clasping at
the base, others narrow and separating and have entire margins. The inflorescence is a long
spike about one metre in length. The flowers grow in panicles. The inflorescence is 30-60
cm long (Figure 3). The arrangement of the flowers of this fragrant grass gives it the name
‘Cymbopogon’ (Saxena and Brahmam 1996; Wifek et al. 2016).
Phytochemical constituents of Lemongrass: The analysis of Cymbopogon through
qualitative and quantitative phytochemical screening has identified significant bioactive
chemical compounds that may be associated with the plant's therapeutic effectiveness.
Various bioactive constituents, including ketones, alcohols, phenols, terpenes, flavonoids,
saponins, steroids, tannins, alkaloids, terpenoids, polyphenols, esters, aldehyde, and fatty
acids, have been identified. According to research, the most crucial compounds found in
Cymbopogon are essential oil and flavonoids, which play a key role in the plant's therapeutic
and pharmacological properties (Asif and Khodadadi 2013; Roriz et al. 2014; Zannou et al.
2015). The primary source of essential oil extracted from lemongrass is predominantly
located in the plant's leaves, which contain up to 5 % dry weight of essential oils. The main
component, citral, gives the oil a unique lemon-like fragrance (Kaini et al. 2022).

Economic importance: The leaves are the source of the essential oil, which contains over
70% citral. This citral is a key component for the synthesis of B‐ionones. Additionally, the
oil includes more than 17% oleoresin. Citral is widely used in perfumery for various
products such as soaps, detergents, cosmetics, insect repellents, room fresheners, ayurvedic
preparations, and flavouring agents for soft drinks and tea. The oil, rich in citral, possesses
germicidal, medicinal, and flavouring properties. Therefore, cultivating lemon grass not
only ensures a steady income for farm families year-round but also fills open spaces in
plantations. Due to its ease of cultivation, low risk, minimal investment, and guaranteed
market and income. In addition to the domestic market, lemongrass oil also has significant
export potential. Currently, Sri Lanka and Indonesia are the top exporters of these oils (Joy
et al. 2006; Sharma et al. 2022). Lemongrass is cultivated worldwide, with the main sources
of essential oil being India and Guatemala. The East Indian variety from India contains a
higher citral content compared to the West Indies variety from Guatemala. India, as the
leading global producer of lemongrass, contributes nearly 80% of the total output, with a
monthly market flow of 45 to 55 MT. Indian lemongrass is typically harvested three times
a year, but some well-maintained crops can yield up to 5 harvests annually. It is important
to note that leasing land accounts for approximately 25-30% of the total production costs in
India. This is significant due to the increasing population in India, which has led to the

Traditional importance: In the continents of Asia, South America, and Africa, the leaves
have traditionally been utilized as tea or decoction. These leaves contain essential bioactive
compounds that determine the plant's various ethno-medicinal properties. Additionally, they
are employed as deodorants in numerous products such as perfume, local soaps, candles,
and other insect repellents. Moreover, in certain regions of Asia and African countries, they
have been employed as snake and reptile repellents (Oladeji et al. 2020).
Food values: Dried lemongrass leaves are widely used as a lemon flavour ingredient in
herbal teas, prepared either by decoction or infusion of 2-3 leaves in 250 or 500 ml of water
and other formulations. Lemongrass tea is a diuretic and imparts no biochemical changes to
the body in comparison with the ordinary tea. Lemongrass iced tea is prepared by steeping
several stalks in a few quarts of boiling water. This can also be combined with green or black
teas. Lemongrass is commonly used in Asian cookery. When Thai food was embraced in

the US, lemongrass became a household name. A simple syrup made by steeping lemongrass
in a mix of equal parts hot water and sugar can be used to enhance fruit salads (Joy et al.

candy baked foods, gelatines, and puddings, meat and meat products and fat and oils. It is
used to improve the flavour of some fish, and can be used to flavour wines, sauces etc.
Lemongrass oleoresin is mainly used in flavouring foods, drinks, and bakery preparations
(Joy et al. 2006; Thasrin and Anitha 2023).
Nutraceutical potentials of Lemongrass: Lemongrass can be used either fresh or dried and
powdered as a food flavouring. It is commonly added to teas, soups, and curries, and can be
paired with poultry, fish, beef, and seafood. Numerous studies have highlighted the
therapeutic benefits of lemongrass leaves and other parts when infused to alleviate nausea,
stomach aches, constipation, prevent ulcers, and combat various stomach infections.
Lemongrass essential oil is a volatile and chemically lipophilic thick yellow liquid. It
primarily consists of monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and phenylpropanoids. The main
component, citral, is composed of geranial and neral isomers and serves as a precursor to
produce alpha-, beta-, and beta-carotene. Beta ionone is utilized in the synthesis of vitamin
A, while alpha ionone finds applications in flavors, cosmetics, and fragrances (Fokom et al.
2019). Moreover, lemongrass essential oil exhibits potential antibacterial, anti-
inflammatory, analgesic, antioxidant, antispasmodic, antipyretic, diuretic, insecticidal,
larvicidal, pesticidal, antifeedant activities, and sedative properties. It is not only used as a
spice for its pleasant taste but also plays a role in food preservation and processing
(Boukhatem et al. 2014; Barbosa et al. 2008). The primary chemical constituents found in
Cymbopogon citratus are terpenes, alcohols, ketones, aldehydes, and esters. Additionally, it
contains other phytoconstituents such as Terpinolene, Geranyl acetate, Citral, Nerol
Geraniol, Citronellal, Myrecene, and Terpinol Methyl heptenone, which are present in its
essential oils. Furthermore, the plant has been identified to contain phenolic compounds like
luteolin, isoorientin 2'-O-rhamnoside, quercetin, kaem (Thasrin and Anitha 2023).

Medicinal uses: Less reports are available on medicinal grass species (Mishra et al. 2022;
Nayak and Kumar 2023). In this aspect, documentation of medicinal uses of lemongrass is
an important work. Lemongrass essential oil has been utilized as medicine for centuries to
enhance circulation, regulate menstrual cycles, aid digestion, and boost immunity. It is also
employed in the production of perfumes, flavors, detergents, and pharmaceuticals
(Majewska et al. 2019). It offers numerous benefits in preventing gastrointestinal disorders,
including gastric ulcers, as well as aiding in bowel stimulation and improving digestion. Its
anti-inflammatory properties are also effective in treating constipation, ulcerative colitis,
diarrhoea, nausea, and stomach aches (Madane 2024). Moreover, lemongrass plays a
significant role in promoting better sleep by calming the muscles and nerves, thus inducing
deep sleep. It is widely utilized in Ayurvedic medicine for its healing effects on respiratory
disorders such as coughs and colds. Its vitamin C content, combined with other beneficial
components, provides relief from nasal blockages, flu, and bronchial asthma, among other
respiratory ailments (Hanif et al. 2010; Khan et al. 2012). Lemongrass is recognized as a
febrifuge and is commonly referred to as 'fever grass' due to its positive impact on reducing
fevers. The antipyretic and diaphoretic properties of lemongrass are widely utilized in
Ayurvedic medicine to treat fevers by promoting sweating. Lemongrass acts as an antiseptic
and is effective in addressing infections like ringworm, sores, Athlete's Foot, scabies, and
urinary tract infections because of its antimicrobial and anti-fungal characteristics (Coelho
et al. 2016). Lemongrass aids in relieving pain and discomfort associated with headaches
and migraines, enhance blood circulation, and help alleviate spasms, muscle cramps, sprains,
and backaches. Lemongrass is beneficial in treating sports injuries, including dislocations,
internal injuries, and bruises. It also helps in stimulation of the mind and assists in managing
convulsions, nervousness, vertigo, and various neuronal disorders such as Alzheimer's and
Parkinson's disease. Lemongrass is utilized in therapeutic baths to calm the nerves and
alleviate symptoms of anxiety and fatigue induced by stress (Wifek et al. 2016; Madane
2024).
Conclusion: Medicinal herbs have significantly enhanced the quality of primary healthcare
by providing herbal drugs that are free from any adverse health effects. The evaluation of
various plants used in India's traditional therapeutic system is gaining popularity in the field
of pharmacology. Lemongrass, known for its valuable essential oils and its extensive use in
traditional medicine and culinary practices, is becoming increasingly popular. Given the
growing interest in natural products derived from lemongrass, a comprehensive analysis of
