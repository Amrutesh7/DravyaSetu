Neem (Azadirachta indica A. Juss): a multifaceted tree & an elixir 
in the traditional system of Indian medicine

Abstract
Neem, called ‘Arista’ in Sanskrit, meaning ‘reliever of sickness’, often referred to as ‘the bitter gem’, is one of the most cel-
ebrated plants in the Indian Traditional Healthcare system for its diverse medicinal properties. Scientifically, Azadirachta 
indica, is classified in the Mahogany (Meliaceae) family. Neem stands out as a beacon of promise for the twenty-first 
century. Each part of the Neem tree, i.e., its flowers, leaves, fruits, seed, seed oil, Neem cake, and gum, has therapeutic 
properties. Researchers have identified nearly 300 distinct phytochemicals within the Neem tree, having diverse phar-
macological effects like antimicrobial, antiviral, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, anti-plasmodial, antiseptic, antipyretic, 
anti-diabetic, and anti-ulcer activities thereby justifying the symbolic term ‘elixir’ written in the title of this review. Neem 
is mentioned in ancient Ayurvedic treatises like Charaka Samhita, Susruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridaya (a book authored 
by Vaghbata) and in nighantus (medical lexicon) written over the ages. In Ayurveda, plenty of formulations contain Neem 
as an ingredient. This review sheds light on Azadirachta indica A. Juss (Neem plant) from the standpoint of the traditional 
system of Indian medicine vis-à-vis its taxonomical position, macroscopic and organoleptic characteristics, bio-activities, 
use as bio-pesticide and bio-fertilizer, active phytoconstituents, and pharmacological studies. Additionally, it presents 
a compilation of folklore recipes and Ayurvedic, Homeopathic, Siddha and Unani formulations (ASU & H) that contain 
Neem as an ingredient.

1  Introduction
Medicinal plants are one of the nature’s invaluable gifts and have been used from the remote past to treat various human 
diseases across the globe. In rural areas of underdeveloped and developing countries, approximately 80% of people rely 
on plant-based extracts, i.e., traditional medicine, which serves as the primary source of healthcare needs [1]. One of the 
key issues with modern drugs is their side effects, which has significantly contributed to a growing interest in traditional 
medicine like Ayurveda, and a surge in the use of herbal products in recent years has also been observed [2]. Plants also 
provide essential nutrients, including macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, and fats), micronutrients (minerals and 
vitamins) and secondary metabolites (alkaloids, phenolics, tannins, flavonoids, etc.), all of which have beneficial effects 
on human health [3].
 The Neem (Azadirachta indica) is one of the most celebrated, multifaceted and versatile medicinal plants known for 
its numerous health benefits. Tons of benefits and therapeutic properties associated with the Neem plant have been 
documented in hymns (shloka) and texts from time immemorial [4 ]. The shloka (Sanskrit verse) (see footnote) depicting 
the health benefits of the Neem (A. indica) plant is just an indicative one. 1﻿ The mention of A. indica (Sanskrit: Nimba or 
﻿Picumaradah, commonly called Neem or Indian lilac) can be found in ancient Ayurvedic texts comprising Charaka Sam-
hita, Susruta Samhita (written during 1500 B.C.–400 A.D. by Acharya Charaka and Acharya Susruta respectively), book 
(Ashtanga Hridaya) authored by Vaghbata (~ 500 AD) as well as in nighantus (medical lexicon) written across the ages. 
Trans-Disciplinary University, Bengaluru’s bibliographic database highlights that ‘Nimba’ is extensively documented in 
Ayurvedic scriptures, with 448 mentions across 20 foundational texts dated between 1500 BC and 1900 AD [5 ]. In Sanskrit, 
the Neem tree is called ‘Arista’, meaning ‘reliever of sickness’, or ‘sarba-rogaribarini’, which means it has the potency to 
alleviate diseases [6 ]. Its depiction is found not only in Nighantus (Dhanvantari Nighantu [7 ], Bhavprakash Nighantu [8 ], 
﻿Priya Nighantu, Kaidev Nighantu [9 ], Dravyaguna Nighantu [10 ]) but also in Vedic texts (Atharva Veda) [11 ], epics and 
Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API) [12 ]. For example, the Ramayana, dating back to the Tretayuga around 3000 BC, 
lists Nimba as one of the significant trees in Nandavana. The neem tree, as it is commonly known, is celebrated in various 
classical works, including the Brhat Samhita and Padma Purana. Astrologically, Neem is recommended as a protective 
measure for those born under the star Purvabhadra. Ayurveda attributes over 50 different Sanskrit names to the Neem 
tree, reflecting its diverse morphological and therapeutic characteristics [5 ]. It is called ‘Kakaphala’ because crows con-
sume its fruits. Neem is recognised for inducing vomiting, yet it is also an antiemetic, earning the name ‘Chardighna’. It’s 
called ‘Hingu Niryasa’ due to the gum it secretes, resembling asafetida. Its anti-inflammatory abilities are captured in the 
name ‘Puyari’, with ‘puya’ meaning pus and ‘ari’ meaning enemy. ‘Krimighna’ underscores its effectiveness against worms 
and infections, while ‘Picumarda’ relates to its skin-healing powers [13 ]. The Charaka Samhita details Neem’s applications 
for skin conditions, diabetes, and more. Interestingly, Charaka’s emphasis on Neem’s dermatological and metabolic 
benefits differs from that of other scholars, such as Susruta, who prioritises its anti-inflammatory effects. Susruta also 
endorses Neem as the optimal choice for oral hygiene, particularly for brushing teeth, and as a treatment for nadivrana, 
a type of ulcerative vascular disease [14 ]. The Neem tree is well known for providing an extensive range of non-timber 
products, including its leaves, bark, flowers, fruit, seeds, gum, oil, and Neem cake. The economic and ecological benefits 
are significant because the tree does not need to be felled to harvest these valuable resources. Apart from the traditional 
medicine system, in the modern era, Neem is celebrated for its diverse medicinal properties, such as anti-allergenic, 
anti-inflammatory, antifeedant, and anti-fungal, and acts as an antiseptic, scabicide, heart tonic, diuretic, insecticide, 
larvicide, nematicide, and spermicide etc. [15 , 16 ]. These attributes have made Neem a highly sought-after plant in vari-
ous applications [17 ]. Besides, Neem oil is used as a holistic pesticide and has widespread applications in crop protection 
[18 ]. In addition, in recent years, the incorporation of Neem or Neem derived products into skincare and cosmetics has

4  Neem tree at a glance
The evergreen species Neem tree can grow to medium or large sizes up to 15 to 20 m or even taller when conditions are 
favourable. It grows across the flatlands of the Indian subcontinent up to an elevation of 900 m [12, 27]. It is also found 
in Southeast Asian countries, the African continent, the Caribbean islands, and Central and South American countries 
[28]. Neem trees can survive well in dry conditions and are cultivated to combat soil erosion and promote conservation. 
For the proper growth of the Neem tree, pH is required to be between 4 and 10. It enhances the soil’s nutrient content 
and water retention capacity, can neutralise acidic soils, and is beneficial in reviving deteriorated lands [29, 30].
In Ayurveda, the Neem tree is utilised for skin care and enhances the skin’s beauty [31]. The Neem tree is one of the 
most multifaceted, versatile and potent plants grown in tropical regions. It offers the most significant number of ben-
eficial non-timber products, which includes leaves, bark, flowers, fruit, seeds, gum, oil, and Neem cake, more than any 
other tree species, which is beneficial as well as economical from the angle is that the whole tree need not be cut for 
acquiring those products. It is recognised for a wide array of medicinal properties, i.e., anti-allergenic, anti-inflammatory, 
antifeedant, and anti-fungal, and acts as an antiseptic, scabicide, heart tonic, diuretic, insecticide, larvicide, nematicide, 
and spermicide. These properties have led to widespread use, establishing Neem as a valuable ecological asset [32, 33].

5  Folklore about Neem
In the Indian context and as per Hindu mythology, the Neem tree is symbolised by the Goddess Durga, the wife of Lord 
Shiva, commonly known as Devi Parvati. In some regions of India, the Neem tree is considered a divine entity. Neemari 
Devi, whose other name is Goddess Sitala Devi in the north, as well as Goddess Marimman in the south, are both known 
to heal skin ailments like smallpox. In Odisha, the famous Jagannatha deities are made up of Neem wood. In traditional 
healing practices, Neem leaves and twigs are utilised in tantra and mantra therapies to protect against evil spirits. The 
Neem tree is believed to transmit the healing potential from the physician/exorcist to the victim. In Ayurveda, Bhoot 
Vidya (Psychiatry) refers to the practice of exorcising evil entities, which falls under the category of Mantra therapy, 
which includes Mani (Gem), Mantra (Spells), and Ousadham (Medicine). Specifically, Neem leaves are employed in mantra 
therapy to treat venomous bites from snakes and scorpions. This is done by reciting holy mantras and performing tantric 
ceremonies, where traditional healers use Neem twigs with leaves on the affected person. Apart from this, Neem flowers 
and leaves are used in Indian cooking. [35, 36].
6  Recipes with Neem
Although Neem is bitter, considering its numerous health benefits, in Indian tradition, it is used in recipes, mainly leaves 
and flowers. Neem Aloo Vaja (Neem Potato Fry), Neem Baigan Sabji (Neem Brinjal Curry), Neem juice, Neem tea, Neem 
chutney (Neem Sauce), Vepampoo Rasam (Neem Flower Broth), Neem Flower cutlet, Neem Narkel Bhaja (Neem Coconut 
Fry), Neem Leaf Pakoda (Neem Leaf Fritter), Neem Sukto (Bitter mixed vegetable stew), Neem Bori (Dried lentil nuggets 
made with Neem), Neem Papad (Neem crispbread) (Fig. 3) are a few preparations made from Neem.
7  Neem as an organic pesticide

ity Protection Act and Worker Protection Standard, which restricted traditional chemical pesticides. This led to a shift 
towards integrated pest management (IPM) and using natural motifs. Neem-based insecticides, notably those containing 
azadirachtin, emerged as a solution. Azadirachtin is effective against over 300 insect species, including significant pests

8  Neem as an organic fertilizer
The Neem tree is hailed as one of the most promising trees for fostering sustainable food production in the twenty-first 
century, according to the WHO/UNEP [20]. Neem products are now used as an indispensable agent in organic farming 
[40]. Neem cake, a by-product of the industrial extraction of Neem oil, contains a substantial nitrogen content ranging 
from 2 to 5% (refer to Table 4), which presents challenges for its utilization as an affordable fertilizer. Additionally, Neem 
cake is abundant in sulphur compounds and limonoids [41]. Table 4 documents the major nutrient elements present in 
Neem cake.
The US Environmental Protection Agency certified Neem-based products’ effectiveness and environmental safety in 
2012 [44]. Furthermore, under the European Parliament’s 2019/1009 Regulation, Neem cake is recognized for its potential 
to enhance the cultivation of high-quality fruits and vegetables in an environmentally sustainable manner while main-
taining the natural balance of the soil through its bio-stimulant properties [45]. In this regard, Neem cake’s function as a 
bio-stimulant is linked to its role in sustainable nematode management, either by mitigating the impact of nematodes

Neem cake enhances the organic content of the soil by lowering alkaline levels and generating organic acids as it 
decomposes. Since Neem is entirely natural, it harmonizes with soil microbes, enriches the rhizosphere microflora, and 
contributes to a more robust soil structure, better water retention, and improved aeration, supporting healthier root 
growth. Additionally, Neem cake shields plant roots from nematodes, soil grubs, and white ants. Neem contributes to 
soil fertility by containing a substance that prevents soil bacteria from converting nitrogenous compounds into nitrogen 
gas. As a nitrification inhibitor, Neem extends nitrogen availability for both short-term and long-term crops. When used 
alongside nitrogenous fertilizers, Neem can decelerate the transformation of nitrogenous compounds into ammonia, 
nitrates, and nitrites, enhancing overall soil efficiency. Neem cake is typically applied by incorporating it into the soil 
around and on the roots of plants [49–51]. Table 5 classifies the quantity of Neem cake required for different crops.
Lately, not only Neem cake but also Neem and its derivatives have been utilized to produce a urea-coating agent that 
enhances and sustains the efficiency of nitrogenous fertilizers in the soil. Due to its high solubility in water, urea’s effec-
tiveness is often diminished through nitrification and denitrification processes. By coating urea with Neem, these losses 
can be minimized, allowing for a gradual release of the fertilizer and extending its availability to plants over a longer 
period. Neem-coated urea is environmentally benign, as it reduces urea solubility and helps prevent groundwater con-
tamination. The coating also decreases ammonia loss through volatilization, thereby mitigating atmospheric pollution.

9  Use of Neem in cosmetics, topical products and dermatological care
Neem (A. indica) is widely used in cosmetics, topical products, and dermatological care due to its antimicrobial, anti-
inflammatory, and skin-soothing properties [20]. Neem is a widely used ingredient in natural and Ayurvedic cosmetics, 
known for its versatile benefits in skin care and hair care. In facial creams and lotions, Neem oil and extracts are included 
for their antibacterial, antifungal, and antimicrobial properties, which help soothe irritated skin. In anti-ageing prod-
ucts, Neem’s antioxidants, such as carotenoids, protect the skin from free radical damage, helping to prevent signs of 
ageing. It is also a common ingredient in facial cleansers and toners, where its antibacterial qualities help reduce acne 
and blemishes [53–55]. For hair care, Neem is incorporated into shampoos and conditioners to treat dandruff and scalp 
infections and encourage healthy hair growth [56, 57]. In topical products, Neem oil is applied directly to the skin to treat 
conditions like eczema, psoriasis, and acne due to its anti-inflammatory and antiseptic effects. It is also found in oint-
ments and creams for managing fungal infections, wounds, and ulcers, as it supports healing and prevents secondary 
infections. Lip balms often include Neem extract to repair dry and cracked lips [19, 58]. Neem is also recognized for its 
dermatological benefits. It effectively treats acne by reducing sebum production and eliminating acne-causing bacteria, 
preventing future breakouts. Its moisturizing, anti-inflammatory, and antifungal properties make it valuable in managing 
chronic skin conditions such as eczema and psoriasis, as it soothes itching, reduces inflammation, and keeps the skin 
hydrated [59, 60]. Neem also aids in faster wound healing by promoting collagen production and lowering the risk of 
infection [61]. Its antifungal qualities are beneficial for treating infections like athlete’s foot, ringworm, and candidiasis. 
Additionally, Neem’s anti-inflammatory properties help reduce redness, swelling, and irritation from allergic reactions, 
dermatitis, or skin injuries [62, 63]. Neem is often combined with other natural ingredients like turmeric, sandalwood, 
and aloe vera in traditional and modern skincare formulations [64].

Neem tree is known as ’the village pharmacy’ in India. Various parts of the tree, such as the bark, leaves, flowers, fruits, 
seeds, and oil, are utilized for diverse therapeutic purposes. Neem is a key component in many polyherbal formulations 
in the traditional system of Indian medicine and is also widely used by practitioners of Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, and

12  Safety and toxicities associated with Neem
Evaluating the toxicities of natural compounds is critical before their use in health management. Various animal model 
studies and clinical trials have confirmed that Neem is safe at specific doses. However, Neem and its components have 
also demonstrated toxic or adverse effects. Regular use of neem extracts or compounds has been reported to pose safety 
concerns, potentially leading to toxicity and, in severe cases, even lethality [100]. Several studies have reported that Neem 
oil poisoning in children can lead to symptoms such as vomiting, hepatic toxicity, metabolic acidosis, and encephalopathy 
[15, 101]. Another study using a rat model found that administering Neem leaf sap produced an antianxiety effect at low 
doses, but this effect was not observed at higher doses [102]. A critical study involving rats revealed that azadirachtin 
did not exhibit toxicity even at a dose of 5 g/kg body weight [103]. Furthermore, a study conducted on rabbits to assess 
the toxicological impact indicated a steady increase in body weight in both the test and control animals, with no signs 
of toxicity observed in either group throughout Neem extract administration [104]. A study determined the LD50 for 
Neem leaf and stem bark extracts to be 31.62 mg/kg and 489.90 mg/kg body weight, respectively [105]. For aqueous 
extracts of A. indica leaves and seeds, the LD50 values were found to be 6.2 mL/kg and 9.4 mL/kg, respectively [106]. An 
acute oral toxicity test in mice established an LD50 value of approximately 13 g/kg body weight [107]. NIM-76, a volatile 
neem oil fraction, exhibits antifertility effects when applied before coitus in rats, rabbits, and rhesus monkeys [108–110]. 
Nimbidin has been shown to cause subacute toxicity in adult rats following daily doses of 25, 50, or 100 mg/kg for 6 weeks. 
Its hypo-glycaemic effect has also been observed in fasting rabbits and exhibits spermicidal activity as well. Nimbolide 
and nimbic acid are toxic to mice when administered intravenously or intraperitoneally but are less harmful to rats and 
hamsters. Higher doses of these compounds can result in death due to multiorgan failure, and a sudden drop in arterial 
blood pressure is observed [111]. Nimbolide has demonstrated cytotoxic effects on NIE-115 neuroblastoma (mouse), 
143B TK-osteosarcoma (human), and Sf9 (insect) cultured cell lines, with IC30 values of 27 and 112 μm, respectively 
[112, 113]. Nimbidin, deacetyl nimbin, and azadirachtin are considered nontoxic. Studies on Margosan O, a neem seed 
extract, reveal no apparent toxicity in mammals but indicate some toxicity in rats and mice. Acute dermal toxicity has 
been reported at 2 mg/kg in albino rabbits, though it causes minimal eye irritation [114–116].

17  Synergy in the life cycle of Neem, plants, animals, and humans
The Neem tree (A. indica) is an extraordinary plant that engages with both the plant and animal kingdoms, as well as 
humans, in distinct ways throughout its life cycle. Below, we have discussed the interconnectedness between Neem, 
plants, animals, and humans throughout its life cycle.
The Neem tree begins its life in the plant kingdom right from the germination of seeds. It adapts to different soil 
types and can flourish even in dry environments. Neem contributes significantly to the plant ecosystem, with its deep 
roots preventing soil erosion and enhancing soil fertility by fixing nitrogen. In agroforestry, its leaves and seeds are 
natural pesticides and fertilizers, encouraging healthy plant growth without chemicals. Neem-based products, like 
Neem oil and Neem cake, possess pest-repellent qualities, making them valuable in organic farming for safeguarding 
crops from harmful pests, benefiting the overall plant ecosystem and promoting the sustainability of the environ-
ment as well [27, 178].
Neem trees provide shelter for birds, insects, and animals, serving as a habitat for many species, especially pol-
linators. Various animals feed on different parts of the tree; for instance, birds eat Neem fruits, and livestock may 
consume its leaves, though its bitterness makes it less favoured by grazing animals. Neem’s natural compounds, 
such as azadirachtin, repel insects, safeguarding both crops and animals from pests without the use of synthetic 
pesticides. Insects like locusts avoid Neem-treated areas, minimizing harm to plant and animal habitats. Although 
Neem has insect-repellent qualities, its leaves and flowers still attract bees, butterflies, and beetles, with bees search-
ing for nectar. Ants are attracted to the sap and insects found on Neem trees, forming mutualistic relationships by 
protecting the tree from herbivorous insects. As a result, Neem trees play a key role in supporting diverse wildlife 
and maintaining the ecological balance within their habitats. [38, 179].
Neem holds a prominent position in traditional medicine, widely used in the formulations of ASU&H drugs, with 
humans using its leaves, roots, seeds, fruits, flowers, and bark for medicinal, agricultural, and cosmetic purposes, 
fostering its widespread cultivation. Neem’s leaves, bark, seeds, and oil are used to treat various conditions, from skin 
disorders and digestive problems to infections, with its medicinal use spanning from time immemorial. In addition to 
its healing properties, Neem is a natural pesticide commonly applied in organic farming to safeguard crops. Farmers 
rely on Neem oil and cakes to defend against various agricultural pests. Neem is also widely utilized in personal care 
items like soaps, toothpaste, and shampoos, valued for its antibacterial and anti-inflammatory benefits in hygiene and 
skincare. People use Neem wood to build furniture, kitchen accessories, etc., because of the strength and durability 
of Neem heartwood. Moreover, Neem has deep cultural and spiritual significance. For their protective and purifying 
qualities, Neem trees are planted near homes and temples in India and many South Asian regions [19, 82, 180–183].
When Neem trees die, their decomposition enriches the soil, providing a fertile environment for the next genera-
tion of plants and helping the decomposers (fungi, bacteria, etc.) to sustain in the animal kingdom. Their fallen leaves 
serve as natural fertilizer and mulch, further contributing to the life cycle of the plant kingdom.
Thus, the Neem tree completes its life cycle, right from its germination to death, by aiding, nourishing, interacting, 
enriching, and benefiting both the plant and animal kingdoms, as well as humans, at various stages.

cological applications. Standardization of ASU&H formulations, including extraction methods, dosage forms, and 
formulation protocols, is crucial to ensure efficacy and safety. Rigorous clinical trials are required to validate traditional

19  Conclusion
The popularity of natural products and their derivatives in disease prevention and treatment is rising globally, largely 
due to the low risk of side effects. Neem and its components hold therapeutic significance and have been traditionally 
utilized for centuries. The review delves deeply into Neem’s diverse therapeutic uses and cultural importance, emphasiz-
ing its historical and traditional role, particularly in the Indian subcontinent. Neem’s significance is well-established across 
various traditional medicine systems, including Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, and Homeopathy (ASU&H). Since ancient times, 
humans have utilised different parts of the Neem tree to treat various ailments. Recognized for its extensive medicinal 
utility, Neem treats various health issues, such as skin ailments, diabetes, and digestive disorders, utilizing different plant 
parts—leaves, bark, seeds, flowers, and oil. Modern science has discovered appropriate responsible molecules behind 
the numerous bio-activities of Neem and its different parts. The pharmacological effectiveness of Neem stems from 
its rich phytochemistry, which includes compounds like Nimbin, azadirachtin, nimbolide, stigmasterol, β-sitosterol, to 
name a few. These compounds contribute to Neem’s antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antifungal, antidiabetic, anti-ulcer, 
and immunomodulatory activities, making them effective for numerous health conditions. Clinical studies have verified 
Neem’s crucial role in preventing various diseases. Beyond medicinal applications, Neem is also valued in organic farming 
and pest control, where Neem oil and Neem cake provide eco-friendly solutions. Recent advancements like nanoencapsu-
lation are discussed, which could boost Neem’s efficacy in agriculture by counteracting pest resistance. Culturally, Neem 
is revered in India, with associations to deities and applications in rituals, protective practices, and traditional cuisine. The 
review further highlights Neem’s use in dermatology and cosmetics, where its skin-beneficial properties lend themselves 
to anti-ageing and antimicrobial applications. Overall, the review underscores Neem’s role as a traditional therapeutic 
and sustainable resource, affirming its relevance for health and ecological management. This review article is anticipated 
to build a strong foundation for understanding the phytochemicals present, pharmacological activities, and medicinal 
applications of various components of the Neem tree, highlighting its role and applications in the ASU&H system.
